# How a Brevard Restaurant Used Data to Stop Guessing and Start Profiting

Every fall, the mountains around Brevard light up and the tourists follow. For the owners of a farm-to-table restaurant tucked just off Main Street, that surge in foot traffic should be pure opportunity. But for years, it felt more like controlled chaos — running out of local trout on a Friday night, over-ordering produce that wilted by Tuesday, and scheduling a full crew on a slow Wednesday when three people would have done fine.

Sound familiar? Across Western North Carolina, independent restaurants face a version of this same problem. The region draws serious food tourists — fall foliage season alone accounts for roughly 30 percent of Asheville's tourist revenue — but most small restaurants are still making their biggest operational decisions based on gut feel and memory. That's an expensive habit.

## The Real Cost of Guessing

Here's a number worth sitting with: restaurants waste between 4% and 10% of all the food they purchase, on average. For a restaurant spending $200,000 a year on ingredients, that's up to $20,000 walking out the back door as compost. And that's before you factor in the labor spent prepping food that never gets served.

The Brevard restaurant we're talking about — a 40-seat spot known for sourcing from Transylvania County farms — wasn't in crisis. They were profitable. But their margins were thin in the off-season, and they kept leaving money on the table when it mattered most. The owner knew something was off. She just didn't know where to look.

## Step One: Connecting the Dots They Already Had

The restaurant was already using a point-of-sale (POS) system that recorded every transaction. They had two years of sales history sitting in a dashboard nobody opened. That data held answers to questions the owner asked herself every week:

- Which dishes sell best on fall weekends versus weekday lunches?
- How many covers do we typically do on a rainy Saturday in October?
- Which menu items have the best margin relative to their food cost?

The first thing that changed wasn't a new tool — it was actually using the data they already had. With some simple analysis in a spreadsheet, patterns emerged quickly. Rainbow trout sold out by 7 p.m. on fall Friday nights, almost every time. The mushroom risotto — a staff favorite — sat at the bottom of the sales mix but cost nearly as much to prep as the trout. Two of their most popular appetizers had nearly identical food costs but wildly different menu prices.

## Step Two: Letting AI Do the Forecasting

Once the owner saw what her historical data could tell her, she wanted more. Specifically, she wanted to stop being surprised by her own busy seasons.

AI-connected platforms can analyze which items sell best by time of day, day of week, weather, and season — and flag which dishes carry the highest profit margin relative to food cost. For a WNC restaurant where foot traffic swings hard between a packed October weekend and a quiet January Tuesday, that kind of forecasting changes everything.

The Brevard restaurant started using a lightweight forecasting tool that pulled from her POS data and layered in local reservation trends. Before each week, it generated a simple prep guide: how much of each protein to thaw, how many covers to staff for, which specials to feature based on what was already in the walk-in. It wasn't magic — it was pattern recognition applied to decisions she had been making manually for years.

## Step Three: Fixing the Inventory Bleed

Inventory was where the real money was hiding. AI inventory tools analyze purchase history, reservation volume, and seasonal demand to generate weekly ordering recommendations that reduce over-purchasing and spoilage. For the Brevard kitchen, that meant ordering local butternut squash in quantities that actually matched projected covers — not the optimistic number the chef had in his head.

Within a few months, food waste dropped noticeably. The owner estimated she was trimming several hundred dollars per week in over-ordered produce and proteins. That might not sound like a headline, but compounded across a full year, it's real margin — the kind that makes the difference between a restaurant that survives a slow February and one that doesn't.

## Step Four: Smarter Scheduling Without the Spreadsheet Headache

Labor is the other half of the equation. The average restaurant runs prime costs — food plus labor combined — at 55% to 65% of revenue. Getting labor right, especially in a tourist-driven market like Western North Carolina, requires knowing when your busy periods actually are, not just when you think they are.

The restaurant's scheduling had always been handled the old-fashioned way: the owner looked at the calendar, remembered last year, and made her best guess. After connecting her POS data to a simple dashboard, she could see that her Tuesday lunch shift was chronically overstaffed relative to actual covers, while Sunday brunch — a recent addition — was running one server short almost every week.

Those adjustments came from data she already owned. She didn't need a data team. She needed someone to help her ask the right questions of information she'd been collecting for years.

## What This Looks Like for Other WNC Restaurants

The tools and approaches above aren't reserved for restaurant groups with IT departments. They're accessible to any independent operator in Western North Carolina — from a Black Mountain diner to a Waynesville brunch spot to a Sylva food truck with a loyal weekend following.

The starting point is almost always the same: your POS system is probably already storing more useful information than you're using. Before buying anything new, the smarter move is to understand what you already have.
